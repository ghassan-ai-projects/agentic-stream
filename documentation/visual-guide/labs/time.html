Lab 1 · Stream plane

A late reading cannot rewrite history

Sensors send readings. Networks reorder them. The runtime keeps two clocks apart: when something happened (event time) and when it was heard about. Delay a reading and watch the watermark, the window and the result react.

Reading the picture

Two lanes, two clocks

Twelve temperature readings from the recorded run sit on two lanes. The top lane is arrival order, the runtime's own clock. The bottom lane is when each one really happened. A line joins them once a reading arrives.

Key idea

The watermark is a promise

The watermark is the newest event time seen minus a lag. When it passes the end of a window, the window closes and emits a result. A reading that arrives afterwards is late, and the spec says what to do with it.

Why it matters

Same input, same state

If results followed arrival order, a slow network would change the answer. Event time makes the state a function of the evidence alone, which is what makes deterministic replay possible.

Words used here

Event time
When the sensor took the reading. Windows and results are placed by it.
Watermark
The runtime's promise that nothing older than this event time is still expected. Here: the newest event time seen minus 15 s.
Allowed lateness
How long after a window closes a late reading may still correct it (30 s here).
on_time, corrected
on_time: the result was emitted when the window closed. corrected: a later reading inside the allowed lateness produced a new result.
The readings, their event times, the 5-minute window, the 15 s watermark lag and the 30 s allowed lateness come from the recorded run and its spec. The network delay and the +6 °C spike are what-ifs you control; the recorded readings were not late.
